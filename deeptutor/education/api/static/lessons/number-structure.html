From factors to the
structure of numbers

Finding an answer is the beginning. How can you prove that you have found every answer—and use the same idea in a new problem?

Start with whole-number multiplication, division and rectangle area. Choose your starting point from the diagnostic below; your school grade does not decide your entry point.

Find your starting point

Use paper first. Show your reasoning before opening a hint or checking an answer.

  1. List all positive factors of 24. Explain how you know the list is complete.
  2. “A larger number always has more factors.” Is this true? Give evidence.
  3. How many solid rectangles can you make using 24 unit squares? Count rotated rectangles as the same shape.
Compare your reasoning

The factors are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 24. The four pairs are (1,24), (2,12), (3,8) and (4,6). If both sides were at least 5, the area would be at least 25, so no further smaller side can work.

The statement is false: 13 has only two factors, while 12 has six. There are four rectangle shapes for area 24.

Choose a route. Correct answers with a convincing completeness argument: begin at lesson 2. Correct list but uncertain explanation: begin at lesson 1. Multiplication or division is uncertain: practise one factor pair with an array, explain its inverse division, then return to this diagnostic.

1 · How do you know you found them all?

Goal: use a stopping rule to prove an enumeration is complete.

Worked example · Rectangles with area 36

Try shorter sides in order. Keep only pairs whose product is 36: (1,36), (2,18), (3,12), (4,9), (6,6). There are five shapes. A shorter side greater than 6 would make both sides greater than 6, so the area would exceed 36.

Another method starts with the nine factors of 36. Eight factors form four unequal pairs; 6 pairs with itself. Thus (9 − 1) ÷ 2 + 1 = 5 rectangles.

Try → explain → compare

  1. Find all rectangle shapes with area 30.
  2. Do the same for area 49.
  3. A student says “36 has nine factors, so there are nine rectangles.” Explain the double counting.
Hint 1 · Organize the search

Write a two-column table: shorter side and longer side. Stop when a new pair would reverse an earlier pair.

Hint 2 · Check the boundary

For 30, both sides cannot be at least 6. For 49, do include 7 × 7.

Solutions and reasoning

30: (1,30), (2,15), (3,10), (5,6), so four. 49: (1,49), (7,7), so two. For 36, choosing 2 as a side and choosing 18 as a side gives the same rectangle after rotation; the square appears only once.

2 · Replace trial and error with structure

Goal: explain why prime factors generate all factors without repetition.

72 = 2³ × 3².

A factor can contain 0, 1, 2 or 3 copies of 2, independently of 0, 1 or 2 copies of 3. The table shows every choice.

There are 4 × 3 = 12 factors. Every factor uses only the available primes and no more copies than 72 contains. Different exponent choices give different prime factorizations, so no factor is repeated.

Challenge the method

A student writes 36 = 4 × 9 and chooses either zero or one copy of each factor, producing 1, 4, 9 and 36. Why is this incomplete?

Inspect the missing structure

4 and 9 are composite. The choices leave out 2, 3, 6, 12 and 18. Start instead from 2² × 3², giving (2 + 1)(2 + 1) = 9 factors.

Explain why every positive square number has an odd number of positive factors. Can you give both a pairing argument and a prime-factor argument?

Two explanations

Every factor pairs with a different partner except the square root, which pairs with itself: an even number plus one. In a prime factorization of a square, every exponent is even; increasing each exponent by one gives odd choice counts, whose product is odd. The number 1 also satisfies the result: it has one factor.

3 · Use remainders to make the smallest change

Goal: solve efficiently and test boundary conditions.

Worked example · Adjust 173

Since 173 = 12 × 14 + 5, subtracting 5 reaches a multiple of 12. Adding 7 reaches the next multiple. These are the smallest nonnegative changes.

Repeat the question for 168. How does the answer change if the amount added must be positive?

Check the condition, not just the formula

168 is already divisible by 12. The smallest nonnegative addition is 0; the smallest positive addition is 12. “12 minus the remainder” misses the zero case.

Find the smallest positive integer that leaves remainder 5 when divided by 6, and remainder 3 when divided by 4. Then find all such integers at most 50.

Hint · Move one step forward

Both descriptions mean the number is one less than a multiple. What must be true of n + 1?

Solution and completeness

n + 1 must be a common multiple of 6 and 4, hence a multiple of 12. The smallest solution is 11. Up to 50 the solutions are 11, 23, 35 and 47. Direct division verifies both conditions. The next value is 59, outside the range.

4 · Transfer the idea to geometry

Goal: connect complete enumeration, optimization and counterexamples.

Make a solid rectangle with 72 unit squares. Integer side lengths only; rotations count as the same shape. List every possibility, find the minimum perimeter, and explain why your answer is complete.

Hint · Reuse a structure

The factor table from lesson 2 gives the possible side lengths. Pair them and compare twice the sum of the sides.

Solution

The six pairs are (1,72), (2,36), (3,24), (4,18), (6,12), (8,9). Their perimeters are 146, 76, 54, 44, 36 and 34. The minimum is 34, attained by 8 × 9. This checks every integer-side rectangle; it does not prove a statement about arbitrary real side lengths.

Reverse the statement: if a rectangle has perimeter 34 and positive integer sides, must its area be 72?

Look for a counterexample

No. Sides 1 and 16 give perimeter 34 but area 16. A conclusion does not automatically imply its starting conditions.

Return later · Independent transfer

On another day, without the worked examples: find all integer-side rectangles with area 48 and their minimum perimeter. Explain why the number of shapes is different from the number of factors. Record whether you used hints.

Check after completing the task

(1,48), (2,24), (3,16), (4,12), (6,8); minimum perimeter 28. Each rectangle uses a pair of factors; 48 is not a square, so its ten factors pair into five shapes. Correct calculation alone is not evidence that the completeness explanation is understood.

Keep three kinds of evidence separate: calculation, explanation and unfamiliar transfer. Discuss your reasoning with a parent or tutor. Opening a solution is useful for learning, but is not an independent assessment.
